# Niche or Audience: How NicheIQ Reads What You're After

You can start a research run two ways — by naming a **market** ("peptide
supplements") or by naming the **people you're building for** ("experienced
tirzepatide users"). Both work. This page explains what NicheIQ does with each,
so you know exactly why your results are organized the way they are.

## We read what you typed, not just which box you used

The form lets you say either "what niche are you exploring?" or "who are you
building for?" — but the mode you pick is a **hint, not a rule**. If you describe
an audience in the niche box (or a niche in the audience box), we still notice and
adapt. You don't have to get the mode "right."

Behind the scenes, the first step of every run classifies your input into one of
four cases:

| What you entered | Example | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **A market** | "peptide supplements" | A product space, no specific buyer named |
| **An audience inside a market** | "experienced tirzepatide users" | A specific buyer that sits inside one clear market |
| **A community** | "Porsche owners", "new parents" | A group that's its own audience but spans many unrelated markets |
| **A very broad group** | "older adults", "people" | Too general to point at one market |

## The research itself always stays broad

This is the most important thing to know: **telling us your audience never
narrows the research.** Whichever way you start, the research runs wide — we
gather discussions, mine pain points, and map the real audience segments across
the whole market. We deliberately do **not** filter the evidence down to your
stated audience.

By default, naming an audience changes only how we **organize** the results, not
what we look at. (We also offer an optional "audience-aware" mode that, when
enabled, *adds* some extra audience-specific discussions to the evidence we
gather — on top of the broad set, never in place of it. It's additive: the wide
coverage is untouched, and no pain or score is ever inflated to favor your
audience.)

Why keep it broad either way: broad evidence makes the analysis better, not
worse. The pains and segments we surface are more accurate when they're drawn
from the full conversation, and you often discover that the strongest opportunity
sits in an adjacent corner you hadn't named. Your audience is a **lens we look
through**, never a filter that throws data away.

## What naming an audience changes

- **A market (no audience).** You get the normal spread of ideas across the
  audiences we discover in the data — each idea labeled with the audience it was
  generated for.

- **An audience inside a market** ("experienced tirzepatide users"). We work out
  the underlying market (here, peptide/GLP-1 supplements), research it fully, then
  put the ideas for **your** audience first under a **"For {your audience}"**
  heading. The rest are grouped under **"Also worth exploring"** — adjacent
  audiences in the same market, one click away.

- **A community** ("Porsche owners"). There isn't a single product market for a
  group like this — they might want detailing, insurance, parts, travel. So we
  treat the **audience itself** as the subject and show opportunities across their
  world, rather than inventing one narrow market for them.

- **A very broad group** ("older adults"). This is too general to focus on — the
  ideas would sprawl across unrelated needs. We still run the research, but we
  flag it and suggest a sharper angle (for example, "older adults + medication
  management" or "+ home safety") so the next run lands on real opportunities.

## We never hide ideas

Framing only **reorders and labels** — every idea we generate is shown. The "Also
worth exploring" group is collapsed by default to keep your audience in focus, but
it's always one click away. We'd rather show you the full set with your audience
front-and-center than quietly drop ideas you didn't ask us to drop.

## Honest limitations

- **The audience label is our reading of your words.** Classifying "is this a
  niche, a segment, or a whole community?" is a judgment call; it's usually right,
  but it's an estimate, not a certainty.
- **We match your audience to what the data actually contains.** We line your
  stated audience up against the real audience segments we found in the
  discussions. If none of them is a close match, we show you every idea under your
  stated audience rather than force a label that isn't there.
- **Breadth is intentional.** Narrowing the research to a single audience would
  starve the analysis of evidence. The audience shapes the *presentation*; the
  *research* stays wide on purpose.
